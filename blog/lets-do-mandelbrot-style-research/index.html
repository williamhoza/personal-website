Let's do Mandelbrot-style research [draft]

Remember the Mandelbrot set?

The Mandelbrot set is the set of complex numbers \(c\) such that if we start at \(x = 0\) and we repeatedly update \(x \leftarrow x^2 + c\), the absolute value of \(x\) doesn't go to infinity. It's a simple definition, but when you plot the Mandelbrot set on the plane, you get mind-blowing fractal images like the ones in the animation above. What an amazing mathematical phenomenon!

The Mandelbrot set has a complicated history. From what I understand, Benoit Mandelbrot was not the first to define the set, nor did he ever prove any deep theorems about it. Instead, Mandelbrot's contributions were to realize how interesting the set is, call attention to it, advance conjectures about it, and produce visualizations of it — all of which are excellent contributions to mathematics.

I bring it up because mathematical disciplines are currently going through a kind of existential crisis. The latest AI models have astonishing abilities to prove theorems and solve open problems. We are finally getting answers to many longstanding questions, which is good, but we are left wondering what we humans are supposed to do going forward.

I propose that in this new era, we focus on identifying new, interesting mathematical phenomena, almost like finding new plant or animal species in a rainforest. We can contribute novel definitions, questions, and hypotheses. It's okay if coming up with proofs isn't really part of the job anymore.

This mindset is a big change for me, and it will take time for me to get used to it, but I think I can get on board. We get to do more than just study and understand AI-generated proofs. We can continue to make discoveries, which is a cool, exciting, valuable thing to do. I think this new job is still a job I can love.

I should emphasize that the proof is often what makes a theorem interesting. I'm not saying proofs don't matter anymore. I'm just proposing that we focus on finding theorems with interesting proofs, instead of spending our time looking for proofs of established conjectures.

Quite a lot of pre-AI research already fits into the paradigm I'm describing. Many papers, including some of my own, answer questions that nobody had even realized they should be asking. I'm proposing that we write more of those papers.

Of course, there might come a day when AI models have superhuman abilities to generate interesting mathematical definitions, questions, and hypotheses. Indeed, there might come a day when humans no longer have any role to play in the development of new mathematics. But I don't think that day is here just yet.
